# Worked example: Kettle

Kettle is an invented budgeting app for renters. It does not exist. The company, the app, the pilot and every claim below are made up to show how the [template](../template/hierarchy-template.md) and the [claim rules](../template/claim-rules.md) work in practice.

Numbers are left in brackets on purpose. An example should not invent statistics, and the claim rules say a figure without a source should not appear.

For this example, Kettle is an app that reads a renter's bank transactions, sorts them into groups, and sends an alert when spending moves away from that person's own pattern. It does not move money and does not give financial advice.

---

## Layer A: Foundation

### Tier 1: Core problem
**Filled in:** Renters with little slack in their budget cannot see that their spending has drifted until the week rent is due, which leads to overdraft fees, late rent or borrowing.

**Why it passes:** A renter would say this unprompted, and Kettle is not in the sentence.

### Tier 2: Category frame
**Filled in:**
- Category label: Budgeting app for renters.
- Market you are compared in: Personal budgeting tools, meaning spreadsheets, bank apps and general budgeting apps.
- Result you want credited to you: Early notice when spending moves away from your own pattern.

**Why it passes:** A renter can place it in two seconds. The result names a trigger, spending moving away from your own pattern, instead of a vague promise like "better money habits".

### Tier 3: Positioning statement
**Filled in:** For renters with little slack in their budget who need to see trouble before rent day, Kettle is a budgeting app that flags spending drift early. Unlike spreadsheets and bank apps that show totals after the fact, Kettle compares this month with your own earlier months as it goes.

**Why it passes:** Each clause can be challenged and answered with a product fact. The last clause says what is different, not just what is good.

### Tier 4: Boundaries
**Filled in:**
- Kettle is: an app that reads transactions from linked bank accounts, sorts them into groups such as rent, subscriptions and utilities, sends an alert when spending moves away from your own pattern, and works on phone and web.
- Kettle is not: a bank, so it cannot move money. A licensed financial advisor. A bill-payment service.

**Why it passes:** Each "is not" is something a renter could assume from an app that connects to a bank account. Each "is" is true today. Nothing is roadmap.

### Tier 5: Naming map
**Filled in:**
- Kettle Inc. = the company.
- Kettle = the app a renter downloads, and the only thing for sale.
- Drift alerts = the notifications Kettle sends inside the app and as phone notifications.

**Why it passes:** A new hire can say what they are selling in one sentence: the Kettle app.

### Tier 6: Category sanity check
**Filled in:** Not a bank. Not a financial advisor. Not a bill-payment service. We are a budgeting app for renters.

**Why it passes:** Each excluded category is one a renter could mistake Kettle for because it connects to a bank account.

## Layer B: The case

### Tier 7: Benefit pillars
**Filled in:**
1. Spot overspending before rent day, because alerts compare this month with your own earlier months.
2. See fixed costs at a glance, because rent, subscriptions and utilities are sorted into their own groups for you.

**Two pillars, not three.** A third would need its own differentiator and its own proof, and there is no honest one. Padding the list would weaken the other two.

**Trace check**

| Pillar | Differentiator | Proof row |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Spot overspending before rent day | D1 Alerts built on your own pattern | P-1 |
| 2 See fixed costs at a glance | D2 Renter groups set up from the start | P-2 |

P-2 shows the groups exist. It does not show they save anyone time, so pillar 2 describes what the app does and makes no promise about time saved.

### Tier 8: Differentiators
**D1: Alerts built on your own pattern**
- Capability: Kettle builds a pattern from your earlier months and alerts when this month moves away from it.
- Which means: You are warned against your own normal, not a generic budget.
- How it works, stated plainly: Alerts use your own spending pattern plus a few fixed rules.
- Shown by: Pilot self-report (P-1).
- Spoken line: "Kettle looks at your earlier months and tells you when this one is heading somewhere different."

**D2: Renter groups set up from the start**
- Capability: Rent, utilities, subscriptions and renters insurance come set up as groups.
- Which means: The first screen already looks like a renter's month.
- Shown by: Product fact (P-2).
- Spoken line: "Open it the first time and rent, utilities and subscriptions are already sorted."

**Tested and cut: read-only access to bank accounts.** A competitor could claim this without stretching, so it is not a difference. It moves to Tier 4 as a boundary.

### Tier 9: Alternatives map

| Type of alternative | What it is | What it does well | Where it falls short |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spreadsheets | A sheet the renter builds and updates by hand | Full control, any category, no cost | Only as current as the last time someone typed in the numbers, and it will not alert you |
| Bank apps | The app from the renter's own bank | Always current, and can move money | Show balances and totals. Comparing this month with earlier months is left to the renter |
| General budgeting apps | Other apps for household budgets | Broad category sets, goals, long history | Categories are built for any household, so renter costs may need setting up by hand |

**Why it passes:** Someone who uses a spreadsheet would agree with "full control". No row says the alternative is simply worse.

### Tier 10: Capability chain

| Capability | What changes for the user | What it is worth |
|---|---|---|
| Alerts built on your own pattern | A message mid-month saying grocery spending is running ahead of your usual | A chance to adjust before rent week. [Not yet measured: overdraft fees avoided] |
| Renter groups set up from the start | Rent, utilities and subscriptions show up in separate groups on first open | Less sorting by hand. [Not yet measured: time to first useful screen] |

**Why it passes:** The middle column is something a renter can observe. The last column stays in brackets because nobody has measured it.

### Tier 11: Anchor sentence
**Filled in:** We flag spending drift early, so renters can adjust while there is still time, which helps them avoid a short rent week.

**Why it passes:** It reads cold to someone outside the company. It says "helps them avoid" and not "keeps them from", because a small pilot cannot support a promise (claim rule 10).

## Layer C: Applied messaging

### Tier 12: Segment messaging

**Segment 1: Solo renters in high-rent cities**
- Segment opener: When rent takes a big bite of the month, a small drift elsewhere is enough to cause a short week. Solo renters have no one to split the gap with.
- What goes wrong: Groceries, delivery and subscriptions run ahead of the usual level, and the renter notices after the balance is already low.
- How it is missed today: Checking the bank app now and then, or a spreadsheet updated when there is time. Both show totals, not whether this month is unusual.
- Cost when it goes wrong: Not yet quantified. Candidate costs are overdraft fees, a late rent fee and borrowing. Measure before quoting.
- Our role: Kettle compares each month with the renter's earlier months and alerts when spending in a group moves away from the usual.
- Proof: P-1, P-2.
- Spoken line: "When rent takes a big bite of your month, you want to hear about drift in week two, not week four."
- Who buys: The renter, who signs up personally.

**Segment 2: Renters sharing a unit**
- Segment opener: Shared utilities and subscriptions get paid by whoever is closest to the account, so overspending is hard to see and harder to bring up.
- What goes wrong: One roommate fronts the utilities, the shared group runs above its usual level, and the first sign is an awkward conversation or an unpaid bill.
- How it is missed today: A group chat, or a shared spreadsheet that one person keeps up.
- Cost when it goes wrong: Not yet quantified.
- Our role: Kettle alerts when the utilities or subscriptions group moves away from its usual level, so the person paying can raise it early.
- Proof: P-2 only. The pilot group did not include shared households, so P-1 is not used here.
- Spoken line: "If you are the one who pays the utilities, Kettle tells you when that group is running high, before the conversation gets awkward."
- Who buys: The roommate who pays the shared bills. Others may join later.

**Why it passes:** You cannot paste one block into the other without rewriting the problem, the workaround and the proof.

### Tier 13: Buyer-role messaging

**The renter who signs up**
- Role in the decision: Decides and uses it daily.
- What they say: "I always find out too late."
- What they care about: Knowing early enough to change something, and not being lectured by the app.
- How to frame value: Alerts arrive while there is still time to adjust.
- What not to say: "Take control of your finances." It promises more than the app does and sounds like advice, which Kettle does not give (claim rule 6).

**The partner or roommate whose account would be linked**
- Role in the decision: Evaluates, and can block.
- What they say: "I am not giving an app access to our account."
- What they care about: Whether anything can move money, and who can see what.
- How to frame value: Kettle reads transactions and cannot move money (P-2).
- What not to say: "It is completely safe." There is no source for it, and it is an absolute (claim rule 9).

### Tier 15: Objection responses

**Objection:** "My bank app already does this."
- What it usually means: "I do not want another app," or "I have not seen what is different."
- Probing questions:
  1. "What does your bank app tell you in the week before rent?"
  2. "Has it ever told you that this month looked different from your usual?"
- Response: "Bank apps are good at showing what happened and at moving money. Kettle sits on top of them and tells you when this month is heading somewhere different from your usual. You keep your bank app."
- Do not say: "Your bank app cannot do this," because it cannot be backed up for every bank. And not "Kettle replaces your bank app" (claim rule 3).

**Objection:** "I do not want to link my bank account."
- What it usually means: "I am worried about control or privacy."
- Probing question: "Is the worry that someone could see your transactions, or that something could move your money?"
- Response: "Kettle reads transactions. It cannot move money." (P-2)
- Do not say: "It has bank-level security" or "It is completely safe." Neither has a source (claim rule 9).

## Layer D: Claim rule check

| Rule | How this example follows it |
|---|---|
| 1 One category label | "Budgeting app for renters" in Tiers 2, 3 and 6 |
| 2 Verbs match function | "Flags" and "compares". Never "predicts" |
| 3 Additive or replacement | Tier 4 says Kettle is not a bank. Tier 15 says "You keep your bank app" |
| 4 Honest mechanism | Tier 8, D1: your own pattern plus a few fixed rules |
| 5 Quality control wording | No tuning or review claims are made, so nothing to check |
| 6 State what you are not | Tier 4 and Tier 13: not a licensed financial advisor |
| 7 Specific nouns | Rent, utilities, subscriptions, renters insurance |
| 8 Banned words | "Take control of your finances" is on the do-not-say list in Tier 13 |
| 9 Figures carry sources | No figures are quoted. Costs and sample sizes stay in brackets |
| 10 Proof at its scale | P-1 is a self-report from one pilot. Tier 11 says "helps them avoid" |
| 11 Customers unnamed | P-1 describes the association and does not name it |
| 12 Competitors by category | Tier 9 names types, and says what each does well |
| 13 Escalation owner | Not covered by a tier. In your own version, name who rules on whether a line could read as advice |
