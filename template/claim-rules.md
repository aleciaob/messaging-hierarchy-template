# Claim rules with examples

Every example below uses an invented company so the rule can be read on its own. The rules apply to every tier in the [template](hierarchy-template.md).

**Kettle** is a fictional budgeting app for renters. It reads bank transactions, sorts them into categories, and sends an alert when a person's spending drifts from their own pattern. It does not move money and does not give financial advice.

### 1. One category label, never upgraded
Pick one approved label. Do not stretch it into a bigger category because the bigger one sounds more valuable.
- Say: "Kettle is a budgeting app for renters."
- Do not say: "Kettle is a wealth management platform."

### 2. Match the verb to what the product actually does
If the product compares against past behavior, do not use verbs that imply forecasting or decision-making.
- Say: "Kettle flags spending drift early."
- Do not say: "Kettle predicts your month-end balance."

### 3. Additive, or replacement: choose one and state it
Say plainly whether you sit on top of what the buyer already has or replace it. Never leave it implied.
- Say: "Kettle works on top of your bank accounts and your spreadsheet."
- Do not say: "Kettle replaces your bank app."

### 4. Describe the mechanism honestly
Say what actually drives the result. If it is part rules and part learned behavior, say both.
- Say: "Alerts use your own spending pattern plus a few fixed rules."
- Do not say: "Fully AI-driven budgeting."

### 5. Do not describe quality control in a way that weakens trust in the product
How you tuned or reviewed the product can be stated, but not in wording that suggests the output cannot be trusted on its own.
- Say: "Alert settings were tuned using reviewed examples before launch."
- Do not say: "A person double-checks every alert before you see it."

### 6. State what you are not when the category is regulated or safety-adjacent
Where buyers could assume a regulated function, say which function you do not perform.
- Say: "Kettle shows where your money went. It is not a licensed financial advisor."
- Do not say: "Kettle guides your investments."

### 7. Use specific nouns
Name the actual objects and problems. Vague category words let every competitor claim the same thing.
- Say: "rent, subscriptions and utilities."
- Do not say: "recurring obligations."

### 8. Keep a short banned-word list and enforce it
List the hype words and vague promises your team will not use. Review it each quarter.
- Do not say: "revolutionary," "reinvents money," "unlock your financial potential."

### 9. Every figure carries its source and what was measured, on the same page
If a number appears, the reader should not have to look elsewhere to see where it came from.
- Say: "In a survey of renters run by Kettle ([sample size], [year]), [result]. Source: [link]."
- Do not say: "Most renters overspend."

### 10. Describe proof at the scale it was measured
A small pilot supports a small claim. Do not extrapolate or imply a guarantee.
- Say: "In a pilot with one tenants' association, members reported [result]."
- Do not say: "Renters save money with Kettle."

### 11. Customers stay unnamed unless written permission exists
Use a description of the customer unless you hold explicit written permission to name them.
- Say: "a tenants' association in a mid-size city."
- Do not say: the association's name, without permission on file.

### 12. Describe competitors by category, including what they do well
Never name a vendor in public copy without review, and always include what the alternative is good at.
- Say: "Spreadsheets are good if you enjoy tuning them. They will not alert you when spending drifts."
- Do not say: "Spreadsheets are outdated."

### 13. Name the escalation owner
When a situation falls outside a rule, the rep sends the question to a named owner before it reaches a customer.
- Example: "Questions about whether Kettle can be described as advice go to the head of product."
